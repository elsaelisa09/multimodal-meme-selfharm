\abstract{Self-harm-related memes present a difficult moderation problem because their meaning is often implicit and emerges from the interaction between image and text rather than from either modality alone. This study presents a multimodal binary classification approach for identifying self-harm memes by combining CLIP-based visual representations with ELECTRA-based textual representations through intermediate fusion. The work is derived from an existing end-to-end thesis study and is positioned as a screening component for moderator-facing review, not as a substitute for human judgment. The dataset was assembled from manually collected social media memes, engineered self-harm memes, and public general memes, then filtered to retain only valid multimodal samples. The final dataset comprised 2,178 memes, including 653 self-harm and 1,525 non-self-harm instances. To address labeling ambiguity in this sensitive domain, the study used ensemble pseudo-labeling with GPT-4o mini and Qwen-VL-Flash under a literature-informed annotation guideline, followed by manual adjudication for disagreements. The proposed model integrates frozen CLIP and ELECTRA encoders, projection layers, and an intermediate fusion classifier trained with tuned hyperparameters and imbalance-aware loss functions. On the validation split, the best multimodal configuration achieved 97.7\% accuracy, 98.4\% precision, 93.9\% recall, and 96.1\% F1, outperforming the strongest unimodal image-only and text-only baselines. Additional analyses showed that balanced feature dimensions, focal loss, and simple intermediate fusion were sufficient to obtain the strongest overall performance, while a Transformer-based fusion variant offered no further gain. A separate human-reviewed ambiguity set also showed that difficult cases involving sarcasm, dark humor, and text-image conflict remain challenging, underscoring the need for human-in-the-loop review in real deployment.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Social media has become a dominant environment for everyday communication, and memes now function as one of its most recognizable multimodal forms. At global scale, social platforms are used by billions of people, while memes circulate as replicable, culturally legible units that combine visual material, text, and shared social context \cite{bib1,bib2}. This communicative efficiency also makes memes suitable for conveying affectively charged or risky content in compressed, indirect, and highly shareable formats.

Within this environment, self-harm-related content warrants particular attention because online exposure may intersect with vulnerability, distress expression, and harmful normalization processes. Prior evidence has linked internet use and online self-harm content to both harmful and uncertain effects in young people, while adolescent mental health remains a major public-health concern and self-harm assessment is recognized as a sensitive clinical and policy issue \cite{bib3,bib4,bib5}. For content governance, the difficulty is not merely the presence of explicit self-harm language or imagery. Instead, risky meaning may be embedded in humor, irony, fatalistic phrasing, symbolic objects, or emotionally charged juxtapositions that demand contextual interpretation.

This problem is especially acute for memes because their semantics are often relational. A caption may seem benign in isolation, an image may appear generic in isolation, yet their combination can imply despair, self-directed violence, or suicidal ideation. The multimodal nature of meme interpretation has been widely recognized in harmful meme research, including benchmark work showing that meme meaning frequently depends on cross-modal interaction rather than one modality alone \cite{bib6,bib7}. More broadly, multimodal machine learning research has long argued that fusion strategies matter because the timing and form of integration shape what kinds of cross-modal signals a system can capture \cite{bib8}.

These characteristics motivate the architectural choices of the present study. CLIP offers a strong visual representation backbone grounded in large-scale image-text contrastive learning \cite{bib9}, while ELECTRA provides an efficient contextual text encoder trained with replaced token detection and has been shown to be more sample-efficient than masked language modeling approaches of comparable size such as BERT \cite{bib10,bib11}. In related meme classification tasks, CLIP-based models and cross-modal interaction mechanisms have shown clear advantages for implicit and context-dependent interpretation \cite{bib12,bib13}. Building on that line of work, this study investigates whether intermediate fusion of CLIP and ELECTRA can better detect implicit self-harm signals than unimodal baselines in a dataset curated for this domain.

The contributions of this article are threefold. First, it reformulates the thesis work as a moderator-oriented multimodal screening study and proposes an intermediate-fusion architecture that combines frozen CLIP image embeddings with ELECTRA text embeddings for binary self-harm meme classification. Second, it documents a weakly supervised but controlled annotation workflow for a scarce and ambiguous domain, combining ensemble pseudo-labeling with manual adjudication. Third, it analyzes the model from a system perspective relevant to Journal on Multimodal User Interfaces: the model is framed not as an autonomous judge of intent, but as a triage component for human-centered review workflows in which ambiguous or high-risk cases should be escalated to moderators or trained reviewers.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background-related-work}

Research on harmful memes has grown rapidly, but its center of gravity has remained on hate speech, offensive content, propaganda, and related abuse categories rather than self-harm. The Hateful Memes Challenge established a widely used multimodal benchmark and made explicit how text-image interaction can defeat unimodal shortcuts \cite{bib6}. Subsequent survey work similarly emphasized that harmful meme research remains unevenly distributed across categories and that domains such as self-harm are underexplored in part because suitable labeled datasets are scarce \cite{bib7}. This gap is not only a data problem. It is also an interpretive problem, since self-harm memes may encode distress through dark humor, symbolic framing, or emotionally indirect language that is harder to operationalize than explicit hateful slurs or direct attacks.

The methodological literature on multimodal learning offers a useful lens for this problem. Multimodal architectures differ in where they combine information, and intermediate fusion is especially appealing when each modality must first be encoded into a meaningful latent representation before cross-modal dependencies can be exploited \cite{bib8}. In harmful meme analysis, this has led to systems that go beyond independent image and text processing. MOMENTA, for example, integrates global and local multimodal cues to improve harmful meme understanding \cite{bib14}. Hate-CLIPper further showed that explicit interactions among CLIP-derived representations can improve hateful meme classification \cite{bib12}. More recent CLIP-based work such as MemeCLIP has reinforced the value of vision-language representations for multi-aspect meme interpretation in real-world settings \cite{bib13}. Related affective computing work has also shown that multimodal fusion becomes particularly valuable when emotional meaning is not directly recoverable from a single input stream \cite{bib15}.

Even so, directly transferring these lessons to self-harm memes is not straightforward. The target phenomenon differs from hatefulness in both semantics and practical consequence. Self-harm signals may be clinically adjacent, socially performative, metaphorical, or ambivalent, and they often blur the boundary between risk expression, humor, and general negativity. That ambiguity makes annotation itself a central technical issue. Weak supervision and data programming offer one principled way to expand labeled data when expert annotation is limited or expensive \cite{bib16}. At the same time, sensitive content classification studies increasingly emphasize that moderation categories are heterogeneous, context-dependent, and operationally difficult to define at dataset scale \cite{bib17}. For this reason, annotation protocols and adjudication rules become part of the system contribution, not merely a preprocessing detail.

A second issue concerns the role of automation in moderation and screening. In high-stakes settings, the appropriate target is rarely fully autonomous decision-making. Work on AI-human deferral highlights the practical importance of systems that know when to hand decisions to people rather than force a hard automated classification in every case \cite{bib18}. In content moderation contexts, machine-generated explanations may also help human reviewers, although their utility depends on reliability and task design \cite{bib19}. For multimodal large-model safety, rationale-aware prompting frameworks such as RapGuard have further shown that structured prompting can improve analysis of text-image risk by requiring explicit attention to visual content, textual content, and their relation \cite{bib20}. Taken together, these strands suggest a research niche for self-harm meme screening that is multimodal, workflow-aware, annotation-conscious, and cautious about automation.

This study occupies that niche. It does not claim clinical diagnosis, user intent inference, or policy automation. Instead, it contributes a focused multimodal screening model and an accompanying annotation-and-review pipeline for a domain in which public benchmarks remain limited, semantics are deeply relational, and deployment value depends on supporting human moderators rather than replacing them.

\section{Methods}\label{sec11}

Topical subheadings are allowed. Authors must ensure that their Methods section includes adequate experimental and characterization data necessary for others in the field to reproduce their work. Authors are encouraged to include RIIDs where appropriate. 

\textbf{Ethical approval declarations} (only required where applicable) Any article reporting experiment/s carried out on (i)~live vertebrate (or higher invertebrates), (ii)~humans or (iii)~human samples must include an unambiguous statement within the methods section that meets the following requirements: 

\begin{enumerate}[1.]
\item Approval: a statement which confirms that all experimental protocols were approved by a named institutional and/or licensing committee. Please identify the approving body in the methods section

\item Accordance: a statement explicitly saying that the methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations

\item Informed consent (for experiments involving humans or human tissue samples): include a statement confirming that informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardian/s
\end{enumerate}

If your manuscript includes potentially identifying patient/participant information, or if it describes human transplantation research, or if it reports results of a clinical trial then  additional information will be required. Please visit (\url{https://www.nature.com/nature-research/editorial-policies}) for Nature Portfolio journals, (\url{https://www.springer.com/gp/authors-editors/journal-author/journal-author-helpdesk/publishing-ethics/14214}) for Springer Nature journals, or (\url{https://www.biomedcentral.com/getpublished/editorial-policies\#ethics+and+consent}) for BMC.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec12}

Discussions should be brief and focused. In some disciplines use of Discussion or `Conclusion' is interchangeable. It is not mandatory to use both. Some journals prefer a section `Results and Discussion' followed by a section `Conclusion'. Please refer to Journal-level guidance for any specific requirements. 

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec13}

Conclusions may be used to restate your hypothesis or research question, restate your major findings, explain the relevance and the added value of your work, highlight any limitations of your study, describe future directions for research and recommendations. 

In some disciplines use of Discussion or 'Conclusion' is interchangeable. It is not mandatory to use both. Please refer to Journal-level guidance for any specific requirements.
